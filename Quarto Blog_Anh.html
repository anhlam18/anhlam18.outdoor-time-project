Exploring Outdoor Time and Sun Protection

Author

Anh Lam

Introduction

How much time do adults spend outdoors, and what factors may be related to the amount of time they spend outside? Sun exposure is a part of everyday life, but outdoor behavior and sun protection practices can vary from person to person.

For this project, I used data from the NHANES 2017–March 2020 Pre-Pandemic cycle to explore outdoor time and sunscreen use among adults ages 20–59. I then created a Quarto dashboard to present the main findings.

Questions of Interest

My analysis focused on several questions:

  1. Does the amount of time spent outdoors differ across education levels?
  2. Does regular sunscreen use differ between males and females?
  3. Can education help predict outdoor time?
  4. Does including additional characteristics such as age, gender, race/ethnicity, and sunscreen use improve the prediction of outdoor time?

About the Data

The data used for this project came from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2017–March 2020 Pre-Pandemic cycle.

The analysis focused on adults ages 20–59. The main variables included outdoor time on a typical work or school day, education level, age, gender, race/ethnicity, and regular sunscreen use.

For the sunscreen variable, participants who reported using sunscreen always or most of the time were classified as regular sunscreen users.

Building the Dashboard

I created the dashboard using Quarto and R. My goal was to turn the results from the statistical analyses into a format that could be understood more quickly than a traditional statistical report.

The dashboard was divided into four main sections:

  • Overview: Provides key statistics and compares outdoor time across education levels.
  • Sunscreen Use: Examines regular sunscreen use by gender.
  • Predicting Outdoor Time: Compares the predictive performance of two regression models.
  • About the Data: Provides information about the dataset and variables used in the analysis.

I chose summary boxes, plots, and tables to highlight the most important results without including every calculation from the statistical analyses.

What Did I Find?

Outdoor Time and Education

One of the main things I wanted to look at was whether education level is related to how much time people spend outdoors. From the dashboard, we can see that outdoor time is somewhat different across the four education groups. Participants with less than a high school education generally spent more time outdooors, while college graduates or above tended to spend less time outdoors.

The regression analysis also showed a similar result. Participants who were college graduates or above had lower predicted outdoor time compared to participants with less than a high school education, even after considering other factors such as age, gender, race/ethnicity, and regular sunscreen use. However, there is also a lot of variation in outdoor time within each education group, which can be seen from the boxplot. This means that education may be related to outdoor time, but education alone does not fully explain how much time someone spends outdoors.

Sunscreen Use

I also wanted to look at regular sunscreen use and whether it was different between males and females. For this project, participants were considered regular sunscreen users if they reported using sunscreen always or most of the time.

Overall, only about 20.4% of the participants in the analysis were regular sunscreen users. When looking at sunscreen use by gender, the dashboard shows that regular sunscreen use was more common among females than males. This suggests that sunscreen use behaviors may differ by gender. Even though there is a difference between the two groups, these results only shows an association between gender and sunscreen use. They do not necessarily mean that gender itself causes someone to use sunscreen more or less often.

Predicting Outdoor Time

Another goal of this project was to see how well we could predict the amount of time someone spends outdoors. I compared two regression models. The Small Model only used education to predict outdoor time, while the Big Model included education, age, gender, race/ethnicity, and regular sunscreen use.

The Big Model performed better than the Small Model when tested on new data. The Big Model had a validated \(R^2\) of 0.138, compared with 0.046 for the Small Model. This means that the Big Model was able to explain more of the differences in outdoor time than the Small Model. The limitation here is that neither model was able to explain a large amount of the variation in outdoor time. Even after adding more information about the participants, there was still a lot that the models could not predict. This suggests that outdoor time is likely influenced by other factors that were not included in these models.

What Does This Tell Us?

Overall, the results show that outdoor time and sunscreen use can be related to different personal characteristics. Education was related to outdoor time, especially when comparing participants with less than a high school education to those who were college graduates or above. We also see that regular sunscreen use differed between males and females.

When predicting outdoor time, adding age, gender, race/ethnicity, and sunscreen use improved the model compared with using education alone. However, the Big Model still explained only a small portion of the differences in outdoor time. This led to a potential next step of this project: finding variables that have an impactful prediction on outdoor time.

These results helped us understand some patterns in outdoor time and sun protection, but they also show that these behaviors are more complicated than what can be explained by only a few characteristics.

Conclusion

This project helped me better understand the relationship between outdoor time, education, sunscreen use, and other personal characteristics. The results showed that education was related to outdoor time and that regular sunscreen use differed between males and females. The prediction models also showed that including more characteristics improved the prediction of outdoor time, although a large amount of variation was still unexplained. Most importantly, instead of looking through all of the statistical calculations, the main findings can be seen through the summary numbers, graphs, and model comparison.

Reflection

One thing I learned from this project is that having a statistical result does not always mean that it is easy to explain. There were several times when the results were not as clear as I expected, especially when looking at the regression models and diagnostic plots. The book by David Spiegelhalter did help explain some of those concepts, but the incorporations and intepretations of them in actual studies were challenging. In addition, I did not recall that the Posterior Predictive Checks in Study 2 was covered in the lectures, specifically on how to use it in R. Only the conceptual parts were covered in the book. Therefore, I think more anlaysis needs to be performed so that I could draw better conclusion as to why some of these predictors aren’t impactful and how to choose those that are helpful predictors.
